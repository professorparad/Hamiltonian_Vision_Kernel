# Writing style guide (Siddhartha Patra)

Derived from S. Patra, S. Basu, S. Lal, *Unveiling topological order through
multipartite entanglement*, arXiv:2112.02253. This guide covers the English
only, not the physics. Use it for all manuscript text: abstract, body,
captions, response letters.

Target register: **simple, clear Indian academic English.** Readable by a
student. Plain words, standard grammar, British spelling.

---

## 1. Voice and person

- Write in the first-person plural, **"we"**, in the active voice.
  - "We first study the simplest case ...", "We now compute ...",
    "We have also shown in Appendix B that ..."
- Use impersonal **"one can"** for easy checks.
  - "One can easily see that ...", "One can easily check that ..."
- Use the passive voice only where the actor does not matter.
  - "It was shown in Refs. [33, 34] that ...", "It is easily seen that ..."

## 2. Spelling and word choice (Indian/British English)

- Use British spelling: summarise, generalise, analyse, organise, recognise,
  neighbour, behaviour, labelled, modelling, centre, favour.
  - Keep the -ise/-yse forms throughout. Do not mix in -ize.
- Use simple, common words.

  | Avoid | Use |
  |---|---|
  | utilise | use |
  | elucidate | explain, show |
  | leverage | use |
  | delve into | study, look at |
  | underscore | show, highlight |
  | paramount | important |
  | plethora | many |
  | facilitate | help, allow |
  | commence | start |
  | in order to (repeatedly) | to |

- Words that are fine and appear in the paper: important, nontrivial,
  crucial, robust, essential, unique, simple, general, interesting.
- No slang, no contractions (write "does not", not "doesn't"), no
  exclamation marks.

## 3. Sentences and paragraphs

- Sentences are moderate in length: one main idea, plus at most one or two
  clauses joined with commas, "such that", "where", or "as well as".
- **Start sentences with a linking word** to guide the reader. These are the
  author's habitual connectives:
  - Adding: *Further,* *Additionally,* *Also,* *We have also ...*
  - Consequence: *Thus,* *In this way,* *This shows that ...* *This confirms ...*
  - Emphasis: *Importantly,* *Indeed,* *Specifically,* *In particular,*
  - Contrast: *However,* *On the other hand,* *Instead,* *While ..., ...*
  - Ordering: *First,* *We first ...* *We then ...* *Finally,*
  - Examples: *For instance,* *As an example,*
  - Recalling earlier text: *Recall that ...* *We recall that ...*
    *As mentioned earlier, ...*
  - Summing up: *Taken together,* *Thus, we conclude that ...*
- Put a comma after an introductory linking word: "Thus, we find ...".
- Open each paragraph with a sentence that says where we are going:
  - "We now discuss X in further detail."
  - "We now turn our attention to the case of ..."
  - "Having ascertained the importance of X, we now turn to Y."
  - "Having analysed X, we now turn to a deformation that ..."
- Close a result paragraph with a one-line takeaway:
  - "This shows the crucial role of X in ..."
  - "This confirms the robustness of X against such a deformation."
  - "In this way, we have found that X is essential for Y."
- Avoid em-dashes (—). Use commas, parentheses, or a new sentence.
- Avoid rhetorical flourishes ("not merely X, but Y", "a striking
  interplay", "a rich tapestry"). State the fact plainly.
- Do not over-explain simple things. Do not restate in words what an
  equation, figure or caption already shows, and do not spell out obvious
  steps. Linking words and signpost sentences stay.
- No duplication across the paper. State each fact once, in its natural
  place (e.g., motivation in the Introduction, definitions in the Model
  section), and refer back to it elsewhere instead of repeating it.

## 4. Definitions, abbreviations, and parentheses

- Define an abbreviation at first use, in parentheses, then use it
  consistently: "the collection of subsystems (CSS)", "topological
  entanglement entropy (TEE)".
- Use parentheses for short clarifications, with "i.e.," and "e.g.,"
  (always followed by a comma):
  - "(i.e., the number of disjoint boundary components of the subsystem A)"
  - "(e.g., Mott liquid, Cooper pair insulator)"
- Name a symbol once, in words and in parentheses: "the Euler characteristic
  (χ)", "(denoted by S_topo)".
- Refer to figures and equations in the running text as "Fig. 1(a)",
  "Eq. (2)", "Appendix B", "Section III", "Ref. [33]" or "Refs. [33, 34]".

## 5. Stating results and claims

- Report findings directly:
  - "we find that", "we obtain", "we show that", "we establish that",
    "we demonstrate that".
- Signal a key result with *Importantly,*:
  - "Importantly, we establish that I^N is robust against ..."
- Hedge interpretations and outlook honestly, not results:
  - "we believe that", "we expect that", "we propose that",
    "this suggests that", "this is likely to be relevant to ...".
- Link a new result to earlier work plainly:
  - "This generalises the result of Ref. [33] ...",
  - "This is in general agreement with ...",
  - "We note that a similar constraint was obtained in Ref. [35] ..."

## 6. Section templates

### Abstract (one paragraph)
1. Known background: "It is well known that ..."
2. Gap: "However, the nature of ... remains unknown."
3. Aim: "In this work, we study ..."
4. Main results, each as one sentence: "We find that ...",
   "Importantly, we establish that ...", "While ..., ...",
   "For a general ..., we find that ..."
5. Closing takeaway: "Thus, our results offer important insight into ..."

### Introduction
1. Context paragraphs, with citations, moving from general to specific.
2. A "further detail" paragraph: "We now discuss X in further detail."
3. **Open questions posed as direct questions**, then the goal:
   > However, certain key questions remain unanswered. First, what is ...?
   > How robust is ...? Further, can ...? If this can be achieved, what
   > insight does it offer on ...? Answering these questions is the main goal
   > of our work. We summarise our main results below, as well as present a
   > plan of the work.
4. A subsection **"Summary of main results"** that walks section by section:
   > In Section II, we define ... We then show in Section III that ...
   > Specifically, in subsection III A, we show that ... In Section IV, we
   > demonstrate ... Finally, in Section V, we study ... We conclude with a
   > discussion of our results in Section VI. Detailed derivations of several
   > key results are presented in the appendices.

### Body sections
- First sentence: scope or assumption. "We confine ourselves to ...",
  "We first clarify some important notations and conventions."
- Then build step by step: "We first ...", "Then, ...", "Thus, we obtain ...".
- End the section with a short summary, sometimes pointing to a summary
  figure: "Finally, we summarise the results of this section in Fig. 3."

### Discussion
1. One-sentence recap of the central object and why it matters.
2. Recap of what we did: "Here, we have generalised ...",
   "This has then helped unveil ..."
3. Recap of each result: "Specifically, ... we find that ...",
   "We have also analysed carefully ...", "Further, we have also shown that
   ...", "Our investigations have also revealed that ..."
4. Interpretation, with hedging: "We believe that this is of relevance to
   ..."
5. Outlook: "It will be interesting to see ...", "It will also be an
   interesting challenge to extend these ideas to ..."

### Acknowledgments
> The authors thank A. B, C. D and E. F for several discussions and feedback.
> S. P. thanks [funding body] for funding through [fellowship/grant].

## 7. Figure and table captions

- Start with a noun phrase that says what is shown, then describe the
  panels with (a), (b), ...
  - "FIG. 3. Summary of various results for the N-partite information I^N
    corresponding to different CSS topologies ... Please refer to the text
    for details."
- Keep captions factual. Put interpretation in the main text.

## 8. Quick checklist before handing over text

- [ ] "we", active voice; "one can" for easy checks.
- [ ] British spelling (-ise, -our, labelled), used consistently.
- [ ] Every paragraph opens with a signpost and uses linking words
      (Thus, Further, Importantly, However, Finally).
- [ ] Abbreviations defined once, in parentheses.
- [ ] Simple words; nothing from the "Avoid" list in Sec. 2.
- [ ] No em-dashes, no contractions, no rhetorical flourishes.
- [ ] Claims about data are stated plainly; interpretations are hedged.
- [ ] Nothing over-explained; no sentence repeats an equation, figure or
      caption.
- [ ] No fact repeated from another section (search the whole paper).
- [ ] Grammar is correct (do not copy typos such as "atleast" or
      "sub-sysems" from old drafts).
